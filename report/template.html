Definitions

  • Minutes on the pitch come from on/off times. StatsBomb restarts the clock at 45:00 for the second half, so times are converted to elapsed playing time and stoppage time counts.
  • Per 90 = count ÷ minutes × 90.
  • npxG is StatsBomb's expected goals, penalties excluded; xA is the npxG of shots a player's passes created.
  • Injury withdrawal: a substitution StatsBomb records as caused by injury.
  • Pressure: StatsBomb's event for a player closing down an opponent on the ball, a repeated high-intensity action.
  • Season halves: each team's league fixtures in date order, split in two.

What public data can't do

  • No tracking, GPS or training data. Physical output is inferred from events, not measured.
  • No medical records. Injuries are seen only when they force a substitution; diagnosis and days lost are unknown.
  • No fees or wages. Money in project 1 is illustrative and labelled.
  • The men's data are from 2015/16, the most recent free men's seasons with every match. Teams then had three substitutions, not five, which matters for project 5 and is handled there.
  • Event data are coded by people. Definitions such as "injury" can vary a little between leagues and coders.
  • Every method here runs unchanged on a club's own event, tracking, GPS and medical data, where these limits largely disappear.

The approach

Recruitment as an appraisal, in eight steps

Before the NHS pays for a medicine, NICE works through a fixed sequence of questions. Each one has a direct football equivalent, and steps 7 and 8 borrow from NHS deal-making and clinical lab science.

Health economics (NICE)Clinical science
1
Scope
Who would she replace, and what counts as success?
The comparator is the player she'd replace, not an average player. Success is league points.
2
Evidence synthesis
How good is she really?
Random-effects meta-analysis, players in place of trials, shrinks one noisy season towards the truth.
3
Decision model
What will she add?
A Markov model of fitness and injury, and a league model that turns goals into points.
4
Probabilistic analysis
How sure can we be?
{{ inp.n_sims|n }} simulated versions of the signing, each input drawn from its range of plausible values.
5
Decision rule
What should we pay?
The chance the signing pays off at every fee, and a walk-away price.
6
Value of information
What's worth finding out before we bid?
Puts a price on each uncertainty, so scouting and medical budgets go where they pay.
7
Managed entry agreement
How should we structure the deal?
Outcomes-based add-ons, as the NHS uses when a medicine's benefit is uncertain.
8
Lab measurement science
Which numbers can we trust, and is a change real?
Reliability and reference change values, the tests labs use before acting on a result.

The usual approachThis approach

Why it matters. A club makes a handful of big recruitment decisions a year, each with a lot of money and uncertainty attached. These methods don't replace scouts or coaches. They turn everyone's judgement into a clear answer: what to pay, how sure we are, and what would change our mind.

Project 1, continued

What's worth finding out, and a deal that bridges the gap

Two questions most clubs answer by instinct: should we do more homework before bidding, and how should we structure the fee?

Value of information: where scouting money pays

Perfect information about everything would be worth up to {{ sig.evpi|k }} for this one decision, and {{ sig.voi['Her true level (scouting, more data)']|k1 }} of that is about her true level. So: send the scouts again, or buy more data on her. A more detailed medical would add almost nothing here. It's the method health economists use to decide which research is worth funding, estimated here with the regression approach of Strong, Oakley and Brennan (2014).

Bridging the price gap: an outcomes-based deal

The seller and the club disagree because they believe different things about her level. The NHS faces this with every new medicine, and solves it with managed entry agreements, where the price depends on results. The same idea, built from the model:

The seller's view
{{ inp.asking_fee|k }}
"Look at last season." She produced {{ sig.target_raw_per90|f2 }} xG + xA per 90, a pace of about {{ deal.pace_seller|n }} goals and assists over two seasons.
The club's view
{{ sig.walk_away|k }}
One season over-states her. Her true level is about {{ sig.target_true|f2 }} per 90, so she's worth {{ sig.walk_away|k }} on average.
The deal
{{ deal.upfront|k }} + {{ deal.bonus|k }}
{{ deal.upfront|k }} now, plus {{ deal.bonus|k }} if she reaches {{ deal.hurdle }} goals and assists over two seasons: last season's pace.
  • If she's the player the seller says, they get their {{ inp.asking_fee|k }}.
  • On average the club pays {{ deal.paid_mean|k }}, its walk-away price.
  • The bonus is paid in {{ deal.p_hit_ours|pct }} of simulations, and only when she's delivered: {{ deal.points_if_hit|f1 }} extra points on average, against {{ deal.points_if_miss|f1 }} when she falls short.
  • When the bonus is triggered, the signing pays off {{ deal.worth_if_hit|pct }} of the time.
  • The chance of losing £50k or more falls from {{ deal.fixed_ask.p_big_loss|pct }} (fixed {{ inp.asking_fee|k }}) to {{ deal.outcomes.p_big_loss|pct }}.
  • Any clause can be tested this way before it's offered: appearances, goals, minutes, sell-on.

What it means: instead of arguing about one number, the club offers a structure where it pays more only in the worlds where she's worth more, and gets the deal done.

Project 2 · Evidence synthesis · Women's game

Skill or luck? The truth about finishing

When a striker scores far more than her expected goals, how much of it is skill that will carry on, and how much is luck?

Hierarchical Bayesian modelShrinkageCredible intervals

How it works

Each player has a true finishing multiplier: 1.2 means she scores 20% more than her chances deserve. A Bayesian model estimates how much these multipliers really vary across the league, then uses that to judge each player's season. Players with few shots are pulled strongly towards the average; players with many keep more of their own record.

Same logic as a meta-analysis of small trials: one striking result from a small sample is usually partly luck.

• Players scored {{ fin.league_ratio|f2 }} goals per 1.00 xG on average.

• True finishing skill varies by only about ±{{ fin.spread|pct }} between players.

• Of {{ fin.n15 }} players with 15+ shots, {% if fin.n_confident == 0 %}none{% else %}{{ fin.n_confident }}{% endif %} had a 90% chance of being a genuinely above-average finisher on one season's evidence.

• Khadija Shaw scored {{ fin.shaw.goals }} from {{ fin.shaw.xg|f1 }} xG, a raw {{ (fin.shaw.goals / fin.shaw.xg)|f2 }}. Her true finishing is more like {{ fin.shaw.skill|f2 }} (90% interval {{ fin.shaw.lo|f2 }}–{{ fin.shaw.hi|f2 }}): probably good, not extraordinary.

What it means: value strikers on the chances they get, not last season's conversion rate. A hot finishing season is mostly luck.

Limitations

One season per player: with several seasons, real finishing differences of a few percent may become detectable. Results depend on the xG model, which doesn't see everything (such as shot placement before the shot is taken).

Measurement science: which numbers to trust, and when form is real

Before a lab acts on a blood test, it asks two questions: is the test reliable, and is the change bigger than normal day-to-day variation? Football data deserves the same checks.

Split-half reliabilitySpearman–BrownReference change valuesFalse discovery rate

1. Which numbers can you trust after one season?

Passing and pressing settle in about {{ [rel[0].matches_for_07, rel[1].matches_for_07]|max|n }} matches. Chance quality (npxG + xA) needs about {{ rel[4].matches_for_07|n }}. Finishing needs about {{ rel[6].matches_for_07|n }}, nearly three seasons. A recruitment shortlist built on unreliable numbers is mostly a list of lucky players.

2. How big must a change in form be before it's real?

Reference change values tell labs whether a change in a test result is real. Applied to match data, one match can differ from the last by {{ (cd|map(attribute='one')|min)|pct }} to {{ (cd|map(attribute='one')|max)|pct }} with nothing real behind it, and five-match form has to move by {{ (cd|map(attribute='five')|min)|pct }} to {{ (cd|map(attribute='five')|max)|pct }} before it's likely to mean something. Injuries in matches: what event data can and can't see

How often do players have to come off injured, at what point in the match, and for how long are they missing afterwards? And can free event data measure this reliably, for any player in any covered league?

Study design
Design
Retrospective cohort of every player appearance in four complete league seasons.
Data
{{ I.matches|n }} matches, Premier League, La Liga, Serie A and Ligue 1, 2015/16: {{ I.appearances|n }} appearances, {{ I.player_hours|n }} player-hours on the pitch.
Case
A substitution recorded as caused by injury ("injury withdrawal"): {{ I.injury_withdrawals|n }} cases.
Exposure
Minutes on the pitch from on/off times, stoppage time included. Withdrawals made at half-time ({{ I.half_time_withdrawals }}) count towards the end of the first half.
Analysis
Rates per 1,000 player-hours with exact Poisson intervals; timing by 15-minute period (Poisson regression); position; return to the matchday squad (Kaplan–Meier, censored at the end of the season or a move to another club) against players substituted for tactical reasons.
Bias addressed
Ascertainment. With three substitutions allowed, once a team has used all three an injured player can't be withdrawn, so the injury never reaches the data. Rates are recalculated using only minutes when the team could still substitute.

Project 6 · Exercise physiology · Men's game

A fatigue signal in event data

Tracking and GPS studies show high-intensity running falls late in matches, but a club only has that data for its own players. Can event data show the same thing for any player, including opponents and recruitment targets?

Study design
Design
Within-player comparison in the same period of matches: each player as a fresh substitute against the same player as a starter.
Data
{{ D.men_total.pressures|n }} pressures by outfield players in {{ D.men_total.matches|n }} matches (2015/16, four leagues), with minutes on the pitch in each 15-minute period.
Outcome
Pressures per minute on the pitch: closing down the ball carrier is a repeated high-intensity action.
Analysis
Mantel–Haenszel rate ratio, stratified by player × period × score state (winning, drawing, losing), so each player is only compared with himself in the same situation. 95% intervals from a cluster bootstrap over players (1,000 resamples).
Bias addressed
Confounding. Pressing also depends on tactics and the score (teams that lead sit deeper), and substitutes are different players from starters.
Code
project6_fatigue.py

Results

Players who completed 90 minutes pressed {{ (1 - F.within_match_decline['75-90+'].rr)|pct }} less in the last period than in the first 15 minutes, with a partial recovery after half-time ({{ (1 - F.within_match_decline['30-45+'].rr)|pct }} down before the break, {{ (1 - F.within_match_decline['45-60'].rr)|pct }} down just after it).

Compared with themselves in the same period and score state, fresh substitutes pressed {{ (F.mh_by_bin['45-60'].rr - 1)|pct }} more in the first 15 minutes after half-time (no clear difference), {{ (F.mh_by_bin['60-75'].rr - 1)|pct }} more from 60', and {{ (F.mh_by_bin['75-90+'].rr - 1)|pct }} more from 75' (95% CI {{ (F.mh_by_bin['75-90+'].lo - 1)|pct }} to {{ (F.mh_by_bin['75-90+'].hi - 1)|pct }}). The gap grows the longer the starter has played, as fatigue would predict, and is biggest for forwards on the point estimates.

Without the matching, the gap from 60' looks like {{ (F.crude_late.rr - 1)|pct }} rather than {{ (F.mh_late.rr - 1)|pct }}: {{ ((F.crude_late.rr - F.mh_late.rr) / (F.crude_late.rr - 1))|pct }} of the raw difference is who comes on and the score, not fatigue.

Against the physiology literature

Using video time-motion analysis, Mohr, Krustrup and Bangsbo found high-intensity running was 35–45% lower in the last 15 minutes than the first, and substitutes covered 25% more high-intensity running than other players in the final 15 minutes [4]. Event data, with no tracking at all, show the same pattern in the same direction.

What a club could use this for

  • In-play: the fatigue signal appears after 60' and is strongest late on, especially in forwards, which supports planned changes in pressing roles.
  • Recruitment and opposition: a physical proxy for players with no GPS data. The next step is to test a player-level version against a club's own tracking data.

Limitations. Pressing also depends on how much the opponent has the ball; players aren't randomly chosen to start; substitutes may be told to press; a reliable player-by-player measure is not yet established.
